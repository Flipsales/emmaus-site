How to pray the examen

The examen is a short prayer you pray at the end of the day. You quietly look back over the last few hours with God: where you noticed Him, where you were grateful, where you fell short, and what you need tomorrow. It takes about ten minutes, needs nothing but a quiet place, and works on hard days as well as good ones.

Where the examen comes from

The examen (from the Latin for "examination") was taught by Ignatius of Loyola in the 1500s, but the idea behind it is much older. The psalmist prays, “Search me, O God, and know my heart: try me, and know my thoughts: And see if there be any wicked way in me, and lead me in the way everlasting” (Psalm 139:23–24). Lamentations says, “Let us search and try our ways, and turn again to the LORD” (Lamentations 3:40).

The examen is simply a structured way to pray that kind of prayer every evening. It isn't about grading your day. It's about noticing God in it.

The five steps of the examen

You can pray these in your head, out loud, or with a pen. Give each step a minute or two.

1. Be still and remember God is here.
Take a breath. Thank God that He was with you all day, even in the parts you didn't notice. Ask the Holy Spirit to help you see the day honestly. “Be still, and know that I am God” (Psalm 46:10).

2. Give thanks.
Walk back through the day and name a few specific gifts: a conversation, a meal, a moment of quiet, a problem that worked out, someone's kindness. Small and specific is better than big and vague. “In every thing give thanks” (1 Thessalonians 5:18).

3. Review the day with God.
Go through the day hour by hour, like replaying a short film. Notice where you felt close to God and where you felt far away. Notice what drew you toward love, patience and peace, and what pulled you toward worry, anger or hurry. Don't analyze it to death; just notice. Ask, "Where were You in this, Lord?"

4. Be honest about where you fell short.
Something will stand out: a sharp word, a missed chance to help, a worry you held onto. Name it plainly and confess it. Then receive what's already true: “If we confess our sins, he is faithful and just to forgive us our sins, and to cleanse us from all unrighteousness” (1 John 1:9). The examen ends in grace, not guilt. If something needs making right with someone, note it for tomorrow.

5. Look toward tomorrow.
Think about what's coming: a meeting, a conversation, a long shift, a child's appointment. Ask God for what you'll need. Then hand the day over and rest. “I will both lay me down in peace, and sleep: for thou, LORD, only makest me dwell in safety” (Psalm 4:8).

A simple examen you can pray tonight

If the steps feel like a lot, use these five short prompts:

  • Lord, thank You for being with me today.
  • Today I'm grateful for...
  • I noticed You when... I lost sight of You when...
  • Forgive me for... Help me make it right.
  • Tomorrow I need Your help with...

Then sit quietly for a moment before you get up.

Tips that make it stick

  • Pick a fixed moment. Right after you brush your teeth, or when you plug in your phone for the night. Attach it to something you already do. More on that in make Bible reading a habit; the same idea works for prayer.
  • Keep it short. Ten minutes is plenty. Five is fine. A rushed examen is better than a skipped one.
  • Write one line. A single sentence of thanks each night becomes a record of God's faithfulness you can look back on.
  • Don't turn it into a report card. If all you can do some nights is say "thank You" and "help," that's an examen.
  • Pair it with the morning. A morning quiet time opens the day with Scripture; the examen closes it by looking back. Together they bracket the day with God.

Examen questions for different kinds of days

  • On an ordinary day: Where did I see God's kindness in something small?
  • On a hard day: Where was God's comfort, even a little? What do I need to tell Him that I haven't yet?
  • On a busy day: What did I rush past? Who did I not really see?
  • On a day you're proud of: Who helped make this possible? How can I give God the credit?
  • When you're carrying something heavy: What am I afraid of, and what do I know is true about God?

Praying the examen with Scripture

You can let a short passage shape your evening. Read one of these slowly before you begin:

  • Psalm 139:1–12 – God knows you and is with you everywhere.
  • Psalm 4 – an evening psalm about trusting God and lying down in peace.
  • Lamentations 3:22–24 – His mercies are new every morning.
  • Luke 24:13–35 – two friends looking back on the day and realizing Jesus had been with them all along. It's the passage Emmaus is named after (the road to Emmaus), and in a way it's an examen: “Did not our heart burn within us, while he talked with us by the way, and while he opened to us the scriptures?” (Luke 24:32)

How Emmaus helps with the evening examen

Emmaus is a daily discipleship tool for iPhone, iPad and Android, built to help you understand Scripture and walk it out. It gives each day a simple shape: a morning word written for you, Today's Mission, praying for the people you love, and in the evening, the examen: looking back with God and asking where you noticed Him today.

It isn't trying to replace the Bible app you already have; many people keep theirs and use Emmaus alongside it. When a verse comes up in your examen and you want to understand it, you can ask about it in plain language and be pointed back to the passage. Your journal uses the Scripture, Observation, Application, Prayer headings, and your reflections stay private. Emmaus never keeps score: no streaks, no misses, no falling behind.

Emmaus supports your church. It doesn't replace your Bible, your church, a pastor or a trusted believer. For more on prayer in general, see how to pray, and if faith feels new, a calm place to start is written for you.

Questions people ask

What is the examen prayer?

The examen is a short evening prayer for looking back over your day with God: giving thanks, noticing where you saw Him, confessing where you fell short, and asking for help with tomorrow.

How long does the examen take?

About ten minutes. Five minutes is fine on a busy night.

What are the five steps of the examen?

Be still and remember God is with you, give thanks, review the day with God, be honest about where you fell short, and look toward tomorrow.

Is the examen biblical?

The examen was taught by Ignatius of Loyola, but its heart comes straight from Scripture, especially Psalm 139:23–24, "Search me, O God, and know my heart," and Lamentations 3:40, "Let us search and try our ways, and turn again to the LORD."

Should I pray the examen in the morning or at night?

Most people pray it at the end of the day, because it looks back over the day. Some also do a short version at midday.

Does Emmaus include an evening examen?

Yes. The evening in Emmaus is the examen: a time to look back with God and ask where you noticed Him today. Emmaus is on iPhone, iPad and Android.
