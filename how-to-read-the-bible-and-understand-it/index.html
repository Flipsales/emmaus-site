How to read the Bible and understand it

To read the Bible and understand it, read whole passages rather than single verses, and ask three questions: what did this mean to the people who first heard it, what does it show us about God, and how does it apply to my life today? Pray for understanding, read slowly, let clearer passages help explain harder ones, and talk about what you read with other believers. Understanding grows over time, so be patient with yourself.

Feeling lost in the Bible is not a sign that you are doing it wrong. Even Jesus' closest friends struggled to understand the Scriptures until He helped them. After His resurrection, Luke tells us:

“Then opened he their understanding, that they might understand the scriptures,”

Luke 24:45 (KJV)

That is a prayer we can borrow: open my understanding.

1. Begin with prayer

Understanding Scripture isn't only an intellectual task. Jesus promised His followers that the Holy Spirit “shall teach you all things, and bring all things to your remembrance” (John 14:26). Before you read, ask God for help. A simple prayer like Psalm 119:18, “Open thou mine eyes, that I may behold wondrous things out of thy law”, is a good place to start.

2. Read the whole passage, not just a verse

Many misunderstandings come from lifting a single verse out of its setting. Read the whole chapter, and if possible, the chapters around it. Ask: what happens just before this? What happens right after? Who is speaking, and who are they speaking to? A verse that seems puzzling on its own often makes sense once you see the conversation it belongs to.

3. Know what kind of writing you are reading

The Bible contains many kinds of writing, and each is read a little differently:

  • Stories and history (like Genesis, Samuel, the Gospels, Acts) tell what happened. Not everything described is approved. Sometimes the Bible tells a story honestly so we can learn from people's mistakes.
  • Poetry and songs (like Psalms) use images and strong feelings. “The LORD is my shepherd” (Psalm 23:1) is a picture, and a true one.
  • Wisdom (like Proverbs) gives general principles for life, not guaranteed promises for every situation.
  • Letters (like Romans or Philippians) were written to real churches facing real problems.
  • Prophecy (like Isaiah) often mixes warnings, hope and poetic images.

Asking "what kind of writing is this?" will answer many questions before they start.

4. Ask what it meant then

Every book of the Bible was written to real people in a real time and place. Before asking "what does this mean for me?", ask "what did this mean to them?" Who wrote it? To whom? What was happening? A good study Bible or Bible dictionary can give you this background in a few sentences. The introduction at the start of each book in a study Bible is a great place to begin.

5. Let Scripture explain Scripture

One of the oldest and most trusted ways to understand the Bible is to let clearer passages shed light on harder ones. If a verse is confusing, look for other places in the Bible that speak to the same subject. Cross-references in the margin of many Bibles point you to these passages. Over time, you will see how the Bible's big story fits together, with Jesus at the center. As He said, “Search the scriptures; for in them ye think ye have eternal life: and they are they which testify of me” (John 5:39).

6. Ask what it shows you about God

Every passage tells us something about who God is: His holiness, mercy, patience, justice, faithfulness or love. Before you rush to application, pause and ask: what is God like here? This keeps your reading centered on Him rather than only on yourself.

7. Then ask how it applies to you

Once you have a sense of what the passage meant and what it shows about God, ask what it means for your life. Is there a promise to trust? A command to obey? A sin to confess? Something to thank God for? James reminds us to “be ye doers of the word, and not hearers only” (James 1:22). Choose one small, specific step.

8. Don't read alone

The Bible was largely written to communities, and it is often best understood in community. Talk about what you read with a friend. Join a small group or Bible study at a local church. Bring your hardest questions to a pastor or a mature believer. Faithful Christians sometimes understand difficult passages differently, so humility helps, and so does hearing more than one voice.

When a passage still doesn't make sense

Some passages will stay hard for a while. That is okay. Write down your question and keep reading. Many questions are answered later in Scripture, and some you'll understand better years from now. Not understanding everything yet doesn't mean you can't be shaped by what you do understand today. For a structured approach, see how to study the Bible for beginners.

Questions people ask

How can I understand the Bible better?

Read whole passages instead of single verses, pray for understanding, ask what the passage meant to its first readers, let clearer passages explain harder ones, and talk about what you read with other believers.

Why is the Bible hard to understand?

It was written over many centuries, in different kinds of writing, to people in cultures different from ours. Knowing the background and the type of writing helps a great deal.

Should I use a study Bible?

A study Bible can be very helpful because it gives background, introductions and cross-references. It is not required, but many readers find it makes hard passages easier to follow.

What should I do if I disagree with how someone explains a passage?

Go back to the text itself, read it in context, compare it with other Scripture, and talk it through with a pastor or a trusted believer. Faithful Christians sometimes understand hard passages differently.
