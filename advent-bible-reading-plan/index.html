A simple Advent Bible reading plan for 2026

Advent 2026 begins on Sunday, November 29, and runs through Christmas Eve, Thursday, December 24. That is 26 days. For many people, the season starts with a reading plan and ends with a broken streak and a quiet decision to try again next year.

This page is a simple Advent Bible reading plan for anyone returning to Scripture, or opening it for the first time. One short passage a day. No deadline. No scoreboard. If you miss Tuesday, Wednesday is still a good day to open the Word.

If you want a year-round rhythm after Christmas, see the one-chapter-a-day Bible reading plan. This Advent plan is shorter on purpose: it fits a busy December.

What Advent is for

Advent is the Church's season of waiting and watching. It looks back to the first coming of Jesus at Christmas, and it teaches us to wait for Him still. The point is not to finish a checklist before December 25. The point is to make room for hope, peace, joy and love in an ordinary calendar that is already full.

A good Advent plan is short enough to keep, honest enough to return to, and focused on Scripture itself. You do not need a special book to begin. A Bible you already own is enough.

How to use this plan

Twenty-six days. One short passage each day. Most readings take only a few minutes.

  1. Pick a time you already have. Morning coffee, a lunch break, the end of the day, or the ride to work.
  2. Read the passage slowly. Once is enough. If a line stays with you, read it again.
  3. Notice one thing. A word, a promise, a person, or a question.
  4. Pray one honest sentence. Thank God, ask Him for help, or tell Him what the reading raised.
  5. Stop there. You do not have to add a long study on top of a short reading.

You can read these passages in any Bible you have, or in Emmaus, which offers the Berean Standard, King James and American Standard translations, with every chapter available to hear aloud. Emmaus has no streaks. If you miss a day, you simply pick up the next passage.

The Bible itself treats this kind of waiting as a source of strength, not shame. Isaiah wrote that “they that wait upon the LORD shall renew their strength” (Isaiah 40:31). And God's mercies, as Jeremiah wrote, “are new every morning” (Lamentations 3:23).

The Advent 2026 readings (November 29 to December 24)

The four weeks follow a common Advent pattern: hope, peace, joy and love. The last week is shorter because Christmas Eve falls on a Thursday this year. Sundays are November 29, December 6, December 13 and December 20.

Week 1 — Hope (November 29 to December 5)

  • November 29: Isaiah 9:2–7
  • November 30: Genesis 3:14–15
  • December 1: Genesis 12:1–3
  • December 2: 2 Samuel 7:8–16
  • December 3: Psalm 27
  • December 4: Isaiah 11:1–10
  • December 5: Romans 15:4–13

The first week begins with a promise of light for people who have been walking in the dark, and with the first hints that God would keep His word through a family, a king and a coming child:

“For unto us a child is born, unto us a son is given: and the government shall be upon his shoulder: and his name shall be called Wonderful, Counsellor, The mighty God, The everlasting Father, The Prince of Peace.”

Isaiah 9:6 (KJV)

Week 2 — Peace (December 6 to December 12)

  • December 6: Isaiah 40:1–11
  • December 7: Isaiah 7:10–14
  • December 8: Micah 5:2–5
  • December 9: Isaiah 52:7–10
  • December 10: Psalm 85
  • December 11: Isaiah 26:1–4
  • December 12: Luke 1:5–25

This week names the child and the town long before the birth. Isaiah says the child will be called Immanuel. Matthew later explains that name: “God with us” (Matthew 1:23). Micah points to Bethlehem, a small town with a large promise:

“But thou, Bethlehem Ephratah, though thou be little among the thousands of Judah, yet out of thee shall he come forth unto me that is to be ruler in Israel; whose goings forth have been from of old, from everlasting.”

Micah 5:2 (KJV)

Week 3 — Joy (December 13 to December 19)

  • December 13: Isaiah 61:1–3
  • December 14: Zephaniah 3:14–17
  • December 15: Luke 1:26–38
  • December 16: Luke 1:39–56
  • December 17: Luke 1:57–80
  • December 18: Isaiah 12
  • December 19: Philippians 4:4–7

The third week turns from promise to people: Mary says yes, Elizabeth rejoices, and Zechariah finds his voice again. Mary's answer is simple and complete: “be it unto me according to thy word” (Luke 1:38). Joy here is not a mood you have to force. It is a response to news that God has drawn near.

Week 4 — Love (December 20 to December 24)

  • December 20: John 3:16–17
  • December 21: Matthew 1:18–25
  • December 22: Luke 2:1–7
  • December 23: 1 John 4:7–10
  • December 24: Luke 2:8–20

The last days are the ones most people already know, and they are worth reading slowly anyway. Joseph is told to stay. Mary and Joseph travel to Bethlehem. On Christmas Eve, shepherds hear the news first:

“10And the angel said unto them, Fear not: for, behold, I bring you good tidings of great joy, which shall be to all people. 11For unto you is born this day in the city of David a Saviour, which is Christ the Lord.”

Luke 2:10–11 (KJV)

John later says what those days mean in a single line: “the Word was made flesh, and dwelt among us” (John 1:14). For a phrase-by-phrase reading of John 3:16, see what John 3:16 means.

If you miss a day

Missed days are normal in December. A dated plan can make a gap feel like a debt. This plan does not work that way.

If you miss a day, read the next day's passage. Do not try to "catch up" by stacking readings unless you want to. The aim is to meet the Lord in Scripture, not to finish a chart. Come back without guilt. If a whole week gets away from you, start again on the next Sunday, or skip ahead to the week you are in.

A note for beginners

You do not need to know the whole Bible first. These passages are short, and many of them tell a story you can follow on a first read: a promise, a family, an angel, a birth. If a line is unclear, write down one question and keep going. For help with that, see how to read the Bible for beginners and how to read the Bible and understand it.

If you would rather start even smaller, read only the Sunday passages, or read Luke 1 and 2 slowly across the last two weeks.

How Emmaus can help

Emmaus is a daily discipleship app for iPhone, iPad, and Android. It will not add a streak or a deadline to this Advent plan. You can open the day's passage, read it in the Berean Standard, King James or American Standard translation, and hear it read aloud. When a verse stops you, ask about it in plain language, typing or speaking with talkback, and be pointed back to the passage. In the morning, a short brief can give you a verse, a brief thought, a prayer prompt and one small step, in about a minute and a half.

If you want company, you can invite a friend, a spouse or someone from church to walk with you. Each of you will see that the other read today, not how far along they are, and you share only the prayers you choose.

Emmaus can get things wrong, so test what you learn against Scripture, and bring big questions to a pastor or a trusted believer. It doesn't replace your Bible, your church or a pastor. It is a tool for the season, and for the ordinary days after it. If church still feels like a large step, a calm place to start is written for you.

After Christmas, the same gentle pace can continue with one chapter a day, starting in Proverbs. There is no need to wait for January, and no need to begin a harder plan than you can keep.

Questions people ask

When does Advent 2026 start?

Advent 2026 begins on Sunday, November 29, the fourth Sunday before Christmas, and continues through Christmas Eve, Thursday, December 24.

How many days is this Advent Bible reading plan?

Twenty-six days, one short passage a day, from November 29 through December 24, 2026. The last week is shorter because Christmas Eve falls on a Thursday.

What if I miss a day of the Advent reading plan?

Read the next day's passage. You do not have to catch up. The aim is to meet the Lord in Scripture, not to keep a streak.

Do I need an Advent devotional book to follow this plan?

No. The readings are Scripture passages you can open in any Bible you already have, or in Emmaus.

Can a beginner follow this Advent Bible reading plan?

Yes. The passages are short, and many of them tell a story you can follow on a first read. If a line is unclear, write down one question and keep going.

Does Emmaus replace my Bible or my church?

No. Emmaus is a tool that supports your church. It doesn't replace your Bible, your church, a pastor or a trusted believer.
