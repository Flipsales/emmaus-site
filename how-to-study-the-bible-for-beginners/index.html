How to study the Bible for beginners

To study the Bible as a beginner, choose one book (a Gospel like Mark or a short letter like Philippians), read a short passage slowly, and work through it with four simple steps known as SOAP: write out a key verse (Scripture), note what you see (Observation), decide how it applies to your life (Application), and respond to God (Prayer). Ten to twenty minutes with a notebook is enough to begin.

Reading the Bible and studying the Bible overlap, but they are a little different. Reading covers more ground and helps you see the big story. Studying slows down on a smaller passage to understand it more deeply. Both are good, and most Christians do some of each.

Why study the Bible at all?

Paul told Timothy:

“16All scripture is given by inspiration of God, and is profitable for doctrine, for reproof, for correction, for instruction in righteousness: 17That the man of God may be perfect, throughly furnished unto all good works.”

2 Timothy 3:16–17 (KJV)

Studying Scripture isn't only for pastors or scholars. It is for every believer who wants to know God better and live faithfully. The Bereans in Acts are a good example. They “received the word with all readiness of mind, and searched the scriptures daily, whether those things were so” (Acts 17:11). They were eager, and they checked what they heard against Scripture.

What you need

  • A Bible you can understand. Any faithful translation works. Some people like to compare a modern translation with the King James Version.
  • A notebook or journal. Writing slows you down, and slowing down helps you notice.
  • A pen or highlighter.
  • Optional: a study Bible, a Bible dictionary or a concordance. These are helpful but not necessary on day one.

Step 1: Choose one book

Instead of jumping around, pick one book and stay with it. Good choices for beginners:

  • Mark: short, fast-moving, centered on Jesus.
  • John: deep and personal, written so that you might believe (John 20:31).
  • Philippians: a warm, four-chapter letter about joy in hard times.
  • James: five practical chapters about living out your faith.

Before you begin, read a short introduction to the book if you have a study Bible. Knowing who wrote it, to whom, and why, will help you understand every chapter.

Step 2: Read a short passage, more than once

Choose a small section, often a paragraph or a single chapter. Read it through once to get the main idea. Then read it again slowly. Then, if you can, read it a third time out loud. Each pass shows you something new.

Step 3: Study it with SOAP

SOAP is a simple, widely used method for personal Bible study. Here is how it works:

S: Scripture

Write out one verse or short section that stands out to you. Copying it by hand helps you slow down and notice every word.

O: Observation

Ask what the passage actually says. Who is speaking? To whom? What is happening? Are there repeated words or ideas? Are there commands, promises or warnings? What does it show about God? Write down what you notice before you think about what it means for you.

A: Application

Now ask how this truth should shape your life. Is there something to believe, to do, to stop doing, or to thank God for? Make it specific and small. "Be more loving" is vague. "Call my sister this week and listen" is something you can do.

P: Prayer

Turn what you learned into a prayer. Thank God for what you saw. Ask for help to live it. Bring Him your questions too.

Step 4: Use a few simple tools

As you grow, a few tools can deepen your study:

  • Cross-references: many Bibles list related verses in the margin. Looking them up lets Scripture explain Scripture.
  • A concordance: helps you find every place a word appears, so you can see how the Bible uses it.
  • A Bible dictionary: explains people, places and customs.

Use these tools to understand the text better, not to replace your own reading of it.

Step 5: Hide it in your heart

Choose one verse from your study to memorize. The psalmist wrote:

“Thy word have I hid in mine heart, that I might not sin against thee.”

Psalm 119:11 (KJV)

Write the verse on a card, read it a few times a day, and review it over the following days and weeks. Memorized Scripture comes back to you when you need it most.

A sample study: Philippians 4:6-7

  • Scripture: “Be careful for nothing; but in every thing by prayer and supplication with thanksgiving let your requests be made known unto God” (Philippians 4:6).
  • Observation: "Careful" here means anxious or worried. Paul doesn't just say "stop worrying." He gives an alternative: prayer, with thanksgiving, about everything. Verse 7 promises God's peace will guard our hearts and minds.
  • Application: When I start to worry about work this week, I'll stop and pray about it specifically, and thank God for one thing.
  • Prayer: "Lord, You know what I'm anxious about. I bring it to You now. Thank You for Your care. Guard my heart with Your peace."

Study with others

Personal study is good, and group study is good too. A small group lets you hear what others noticed, ask questions and stay encouraged. If you don't have a group, ask one friend to study the same book with you. For help with hard passages, see how to read the Bible and understand it.

Questions people ask

What is the easiest way to study the Bible for beginners?

Pick one book, read a short passage slowly, and use the SOAP method: write out a verse (Scripture), note what you see (Observation), decide how it applies (Application), and respond to God (Prayer).

What book of the Bible should a beginner study first?

Mark, John, Philippians and James are all good first choices. They are relatively short and clear, and they center on Jesus and everyday faith.

How long should a Bible study session be?

Ten to twenty minutes is a good start. Consistency matters more than length.

What is the difference between reading and studying the Bible?

Reading covers more ground and helps you see the big story. Studying slows down on a smaller passage to understand it more deeply. Both are valuable.
